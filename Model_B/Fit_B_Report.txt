\section{Summary}

Model B (hierarchical over sessions, with the design-based priors described in
the convergence report) was fit to all 12{,}937 trials of the Sirius data
(42 sessions, 4 groups). The main results:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The distractor lowers the PSE.} In the bimanual condition the
        monkey reports ``high'' at lower amplitudes. Averaged over the 42 sessions
        the PSE shift (bimanual $-$ unimanual) is $-4.3$ $\mu m$ [$-5.0$, $-3.5$]
        for the left hand and $-6.1$ $\mu m$ [$-6.8$, $-5.3$] for the right hand.
        Its posterior mean is negative in 40 of 42 sessions for the left hand and
        in all 42 for the right hand, and its 95\% HDI excludes 0 in 23 (left) and
        32 (right) sessions.
  \item \textbf{The distractor raises $\gamma_h$}, the rate of ``high''
        responses to the weakest stimuli, by about 0.02 (left) and 0.035
        (right). For the right hand the HDI excludes 0 in all 42 sessions.
  \item \textbf{The distractor slightly raises the JND} (less precise
        discrimination), clearly for the left hand (+1.0 $\mu m$ [0.5, 1.6],
        largely from a few sessions) and weakly for the right hand
        (+0.5 $\mu m$ [$-0.1$, 1.0]). It has no effect on $\gamma_l$.
  \item \textbf{The PSE shift grows with distractor amplitude} (exploratory,
        Section~\ref{sec:amplitude}): about $-2$ $\mu m$ per 10 $\mu m$ of
        distractor amplitude for both hands.
  \item The sampler converged, the model reproduces the data well, and the new
        priors change the hyperparameter estimates only slightly from the
        earlier fit.
\end{itemize}

Every figure and number in this report is produced by
\code{Main\_Analysis\_B.py}; the cell ``Numbers for Fit\_B\_Report.txt'' prints
the numbers that the earlier cells do not. All intervals are 95\% highest
density intervals (HDIs). PSE and JND are in
micrometres of stimulus amplitude: $\text{PSE}_{\mu m} = 28 + 14.63\,\text{PSE}$
and $\text{JND}_{\mu m} = 14.63\,\text{JND}$, where 28 and 14.63 are the mean
and sd of the 8 amplitude levels.

\section{Model and Fit}

The model is the one described in the Model B convergence report: per-session
psychometric parameters $(\beta_0, \beta_1, \gamma_h, \gamma_l)$ for each
group, drawn from group-level distributions whose means and sds have the
design-based priors
$\mu_{\beta_0} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2)$,
$\sigma_{\beta_0} \sim \text{HalfNormal}(2)$,
$\mu_{\beta_1} \sim \text{LogNormal}(\log 4, 0.5)$,
$\sigma_{\beta_1} \sim \text{Exponential}(1)$,
$\mu_\gamma \sim \mathcal{N}(-3, 1.5)$ and
$\sigma_\gamma \sim \text{HalfNormal}(0.5)$.

The fit (\code{Main\_Analysis\_B.py}) used NUTS with 4 chains of 1000 tuning and
5000 kept draws (20{,}000 draws in total) and took 31 minutes.

\subsection{Sampling diagnostics}

There were no divergences, and the tree depth never exceeded 6. Of the 2{,}080
parameters, all but four have $\hat{R} = 1.00$, bulk ESS $\ge 2{,}210$ and
tail ESS $\ge 4{,}527$.

The four exceptions are the raw variables \code{z\_sig\_b0} ($\hat{R}$ up to
1.73), and they are harmless. The model sets
$\sigma_{\beta_0} = |2\,z|$ with $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, so $z$ and $-z$ give
the same model. The data keep $\sigma_{\beta_0}$ well away from 0, so each chain
stays on the sign it started on, and the chains disagree only about the sign.
$\sigma_{\beta_0}$ itself has $\hat{R} = 1.00$ and bulk ESS $\ge 7{,}000$. Defining $\sigma_{\beta_0}$ directly as a
\code{HalfNormal(2)} variable (as is effectively already done for
$\sigma_{\beta_1}$) would remove the warning without changing the model.

Figures~\ref{fig:trace_sess} and~\ref{fig:trace_hyper} show trace plots for a
few of the parameters that \code{Main\_Analysis\_B.py} singles out: the four
chains overlap completely and mix quickly. This includes the lapse rates of
left\_bi on 05-30, which have the longest right tails of any session, and the
lapse hyperparameters of left\_bi, whose sd $\sigma_{\gamma_h}$ piles up
against 0.

priors predict equally well with fewer effective parameters.

The bias and slope parameters barely change with the new priors
(Table~\ref{tab:hyper}); the group slopes move down by 0.05--0.19, well
within one posterior sd. The group slopes are 4.3--5.0 for every group, below
the steep range ($\mu_{\beta_1} \approx 6$) where the convergence study found
the slope poorly identified.

The lapse-rate parameters change more. The lapse means $\mu_{\gamma_h}$ of the
unimanual groups, which have almost no lapses, move lower (left\_uni from
$-4.46$ to $-5.20$): the new prior $\mathcal{N}(-3, 1.5)$ is wider than the
earlier $\mathcal{N}(-2.5, 1)$ and no longer pulls them up. Every between-session lapse sd $\sigma_\gamma$ shrinks
to 0.36--0.59, close to the new prior (median 0.34). The convergence study
showed that $\sigma_{\gamma_h}$ and $\sigma_{\gamma_l}$ are not identified at
this data size, so these values mainly reflect the prior and should not be
interpreted. The one notable change is left\_bi $\sigma_{\gamma_h}$, which
drops from 1.54 to 0.59.

$\sigma_{\beta_1}$ for left\_bi stands out: 1.27 [0.74, 1.83], far above the
other groups, which means the left-hand bimanual slope varies a lot between
sessions. A few sessions have very shallow bimanual slopes; the JND of left\_bi
on 05-12 is 10.9 $\mu m$, against a median of 4.1 $\mu m$ across sessions.

\section{Effect of the Distractor}
\label{sec:effect}

The effect of the distractor is the bimanual $-$ unimanual difference,
computed draw by draw so that its uncertainty is exact. It is reported in two
ways:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{average over the 42 sessions:} the mean of the within-session
        differences, which describes these sessions;
  \item \textbf{population (typical session):} the difference at the group-level
        means, which describes a new session from the same population and is
        therefore more uncertain.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{PSE.} The clearest effect. The distractor lowers the PSE by about
4 $\mu m$ for the left hand and 6 $\mu m$ for the right hand
(Table~\ref{tab:effect}). The posterior mean of the shift is negative in every
right-hand session and in 40 of 42 left-hand sessions; the two exceptions are
early, weak-distractor sessions with shifts below +0.5 $\mu m$
(Figure~\ref{fig:effect_pse}). This is consistent with a stimulus feeling
stronger when a distractor is applied to the other hand.

\paragraph{$\gamma_h$.} With the distractor the monkey more often reports
``high'' for the weakest stimuli: $\gamma_h$ rises from about 0.002 to 0.02--0.04
(Table~\ref{tab:population}). The rise is clear in every right-hand session
(Figure~\ref{fig:effect_gamh}). This agrees with the PSE shift: the distractor
adds to the perceived strength of the stimulus, enough to make even the weakest
stimuli occasionally seem ``high''. The convergence study found left\_bi
$\gamma_h$ estimated too low in some datasets, so the left-hand effect may be
somewhat larger than reported here, not smaller.

\paragraph{JND.} The distractor makes discrimination slightly less precise. For
the left hand the average over sessions is +1.0 $\mu m$ [0.5, 1.6], but this is
driven by a handful of sessions with very shallow bimanual curves
(Figure~\ref{fig:effect_jnd}, e.g.\ 05-12 at +7.6 $\mu m$), consistent with the
large $\sigma_{\beta_1}$ of left\_bi. At the population level the effect is
+0.6 $\mu m$ [$-0.03$, 1.28], with $P(\text{effect} > 0) = 0.97$. For the right
hand the effect is about +0.4--0.5 $\mu m$ with HDIs that include 0, and no
single session's HDI excludes 0.

\subsection{Dependence on distractor amplitude (exploratory)}
\label{sec:amplitude}

The distractor amplitude was not constant: it was 6 $\mu m$ in the first 8
sessions, 12 $\mu m$ in the next 6, increased gradually to 32 $\mu m$ over the
next 11 (three of them at 24 $\mu m$), and then stayed at 32 $\mu m$ with
occasional sessions at 24 $\mu m$: 13 sessions at 32 and 7 at 24 in total. The model
does not include distractor amplitude, so this section looks at the fitted
session-level effects afterwards, with a linear regression of the session
shifts on amplitude computed separately for each posterior draw.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{effect_vs_distractor_amplitude.png}
\caption{Session-level PSE and JND shifts against distractor amplitude
(points slightly spread horizontally where several sessions share an amplitude),
with the per-draw linear trend and its 95\% band.}
\label{fig:amp}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Mean PSE shift (bimanual $-$ unimanual, $\mu m$) over the sessions at
each distractor amplitude.}
\label{tab:amp}
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Distractor amplitude & 6 $\mu m$ (8 sessions) & 12 $\mu m$ (6) & 24 $\mu m$ (7) & 32 $\mu m$ (13) \\
\midrule
Left hand  & $-1.1$ [$-2.8$, 0.4] & $-4.2$ [$-5.7$, $-2.7$] & $-4.5$ [$-6.0$, $-3.0$] & $-6.7$ [$-8.0$, $-5.3$] \\
Right hand & $-3.9$ [$-5.5$, $-2.4$] & $-4.3$ [$-6.1$, $-2.6$] & $-5.9$ [$-7.4$, $-4.3$] & $-7.9$ [$-9.1$, $-6.7$] \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}}
\end{table}

The PSE shift grows with distractor amplitude, by $-1.9$ $\mu m$
[$-2.6$, $-1.2$] (left) and $-1.5$ $\mu m$ [$-2.2$, $-0.9$] (right) per 10 $\mu m$
of distractor (Figure~\ref{fig:amp}, Table~\ref{tab:amp}). At the weakest
distractor the left-hand shift is not distinguishable from 0. The JND shift shows
no clear trend with amplitude.

Because amplitude rose over the study, amplitude and session order are
strongly correlated ($r = 0.91$), so a change over time (learning, adaptation)
could produce the same pattern. The later alternation between 24 and 32 $\mu m$
partly separates the two. A regression on both amplitude and session order
attributes the trend to amplitude ($-2.4$ $\mu m$ [$-4.0$, $-0.8$] per
10 $\mu m$ for the left hand, $-2.1$ [$-3.6$, $-0.7$] for the right) and finds
no trend with session order (+0.5 [$-0.7$, 1.6] and +0.6 [$-0.6$, 1.8] $\mu m$
per 10 sessions). Split by condition, the bimanual PSEs fall with amplitude
and the unimanual PSEs rise somewhat with it, so a stronger distractor also
seems to shift the unimanual trials of the same session (for example through
the monkey's criterion adapting to the session as a whole). Both right-hand
PSEs also drift upward with session order (unimanual +0.8 [0.0, 1.6], bimanual
+1.4 [0.5, 2.3] $\mu m$ per 10 sessions), a drift that largely cancels in the
difference. With 42 sessions and
two strongly correlated predictors these conclusions are tentative; a model
with distractor amplitude as a session-level covariate would test them
properly.

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The fit is reliable: no divergences, $\hat{R} = 1.00$ and large ESS for
        every quantity of interest, every Pareto $k$ good, and no systematic
        misfit in the posterior predictive checks.
  \item The distractor shifts the PSE down by 4--6 $\mu m$ and raises
        $\gamma_h$ by 0.02--0.035, both clearly and consistently across sessions,
        consistent with a distractor on the other hand making the stimulus feel
        stronger.
  \item The distractor slightly worsens discrimination (JND up by about
        0.5--1 $\mu m$), most clearly for the left hand, where it is driven by a
        few sessions.
  \item The PSE shift appears to grow with distractor amplitude, by about
        2 $\mu m$ per 10 $\mu m$; this needs confirming with amplitude in the
        model.
  \item The new priors change the bias and slope estimates very little. The
        between-session lapse sds now sit near their prior, which is expected
        because the convergence study showed that they are not identified.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Possible follow-ups.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Add distractor amplitude as a session-level covariate on the bimanual
        $\beta_0$ (and possibly on the unimanual one), to test the amplitude
        dependence inside the model.
  \item Define $\sigma_{\beta_0}$ as a HalfNormal variable directly to remove the
        $\hat{R}$ warning on \code{z\_sig\_b0}.
  \item Look at the left\_bi sessions with very shallow slopes (05-12, 05-19,
        05-30) for problems with the data or the session.
\end{enumerate}

\appendix
\section{Joint Posteriors}
\label{app:pairs}

\code{Main\_Analysis\_B.py} also plots joint posteriors for session 06-14
(Figures~\ref{fig:pair_lu}--\ref{fig:pair_hyper}). The correlation of
$\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ follows from the stimulus axis being centered on
28 $\mu m$: it is negative ($r \approx -0.4$) for the unimanual groups, whose
PSE on this day lies above 28 $\mu m$ (36 $\mu m$), and positive
($r = 0.1$--$0.3$) for the bimanual groups, whose PSE lies below it
(25--27 $\mu m$). The lapse rates are only weakly correlated with $\beta_0$ and
$\beta_1$ ($|r| \le 0.26$, mostly with the slope) and with each other. There is
no ridge or multimodality that would indicate an identifiability problem.

Figure~\ref{fig:pair_hyper} looks at the left\_bi $\gamma_h$ together with the
hyperparameters it is drawn from. $\gamma_h$ is positively correlated with
both $\mu_{\gamma_h}$ ($r = 0.34$) and $\sigma_{\gamma_h}$ ($r = 0.29$): a
larger session value is easier to reach when the group mean is higher or the
spread around it is wider. $\sigma_{\gamma_h}$ has its mode at 0 and is barely
constrained by the data, as the convergence study found. The sampler had no
trouble with this region (no divergences).
