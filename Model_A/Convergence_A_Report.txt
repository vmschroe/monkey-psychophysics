\section{Model Structure}
\subsection{Data and indexing}
The data are $N$ binary trials. Trial $i$ has
\begin{itemize}
  \item a response $y_i \in \{0,1\}$, the monkey's categorization of the stimulus as ``low'' (0) or ``high'' (1);
  \item a standardized stimulus amplitude $x_i$. The raw amplitudes are centered and scaled by the mean and standard deviation of the distinct amplitude levels. The covariate row is
        $(1, x_i)$;
  \item a group $g[i] \in \{1,\dots,4\}$: the combination of
        hand and distractor condition,
        \[
          g \in \{\text{left\_uni},\ \text{left\_bi},\ \text{right\_uni},\ \text{right\_bi}\},
        \]
        where ``uni'' is unimanual (no distractor) and ``bi'' is bimanual
        (distractor present).
\end{itemize}
Model A ignores sessions: all trials of a group are pooled, as if every session
had the same psychometric curve. The real data have $N = 12{,}937$ trials, about
3{,}230 per group.

\subsection{Likelihood}

Each response is Bernoulli,
\[
  y_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\psi_i), \qquad
  \psi_i = \psi\big(x_i;\ \theta_{g[i]}\big),
\]
with the psychometric function
\[
  \psi(x;\theta) = \gamma_h + (1 - \gamma_h - \gamma_l)\,\invlogit(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x),
  \qquad \theta = (\beta_0, \beta_1, \gamma_h, \gamma_l).
\]
Here $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ are parameters of the sigmoid, and $\gamma_h,\gamma_l$
set the asymptotes:
\[
  \lim_{x\to-\infty}\psi = \gamma_h, \qquad \lim_{x\to+\infty}\psi = 1-\gamma_l .
\]
So $\gamma_h$ is the probability of responding 1 (``high'') at very low amplitudes, and
$\gamma_l$ the probability of responding 0 (``low'') at very high amplitudes. In the code
$\psi_i$ is clipped to $[10^{-8},\,1-10^{-8}]$ before entering the Bernoulli
likelihood, for numerical stability.

\subsection{Priors}

Each group has its own four parameters, independent of the other groups, with
the same priors in every group:
\begin{align*}
  \beta_{0,g} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\ 6), \\
  \beta_{1,g} &\sim \mathcal{N}(4,\ 2), \\
  \gamma_{h,g} &= 0.25\, w_{h,g}, \qquad w_{h,g} \sim \text{Beta}(\text{mean } 0.2,\ \text{sd } 0.15), \\
  \gamma_{l,g} &= 0.25\, w_{l,g}, \qquad w_{l,g} \sim \text{Beta}(\text{mean } 0.2,\ \text{sd } 0.15).
\end{align*}
The Beta distribution has shape parameters $a = 1.22$, $b = 4.89$. Scaling by 0.25
keeps each lapse parameter in $(0, 0.25)$, so the curve is increasing and the
PSE and JND below are well defined.

These priors predate the design-based priors of Model B and have not been
checked by prior predictive simulation. What they imply:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\beta_0$: very wide. Together with the slope prior it puts the PSE inside
        the stimulus range (6 to 50 $\mu m$) only 64\% of the time.
  \item $\beta_1$: a symmetric normal, so 2.3\% of the prior mass is on negative
        (decreasing) slopes.
  \item $\gamma_h, \gamma_l$: mean 0.05, median 0.041, 95\% range
        $[0.003,\ 0.141]$. This is centered well above the lapse rates of trained
        animals (often below 0.01), which matters for $\gamma_l$
        (Section~\ref{sec:bias}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Derived Quantities: PSE and JND}

\paragraph{Point of subjective equality (PSE):} the stimulus at which a response of ``high'' and ``low'' are equally likely ($\psi = 1/2$),
\[
  \text{PSE} = \frac{1}{\beta_1}\left[-\beta_0 + \log\frac{1-2\gamma_h}{1-2\gamma_l}\right].
\]

\paragraph{Just noticeable difference (JND):} half the stimulus distance between the points where $\psi = 0.25$ and $\psi = 0.75$,
\[
  \text{JND} = \frac{x_{.75} - x_{.25}}{2}
  = \frac{1}{2\beta_1}\,\log\frac{(3-4\gamma_h)(3-4\gamma_l)}{(1-4\gamma_h)(1-4\gamma_l)} .
\]
Both are in units of the standardized stimulus; one unit is 14.6 $\mu m$.

\subsection{Implementation}

As in Model B, the amplitudes are standardized, and $\beta_0, \beta_1$ are written as
deterministic transforms of standard normal variables. The model is fit with
NUTS, 4 chains of 1000 tuning and 1000 kept draws.

\section{Synthetic Data Generation}

\code{Convergence\_A.py} fixes one set of true parameters and fits Model A to
synthetic datasets of increasing size:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
 & left\_uni & left\_bi & right\_uni & right\_bi \\
\midrule
$\beta_0$ & $-1$ & 1 & $-1$ & 1 \\
$\beta_1$ & 3 & 3 & 6 & 6 \\
$\gamma_h$ & 0.03 & 0.06 & 0.06 & 0.03 \\
$\gamma_l$ & 0.005 & 0.01 & 0.005 & 0.01 \\
\midrule
PSE & 0.316 & $-0.369$ & 0.147 & $-0.174$ \\
JND & 0.383 & 0.403 & 0.200 & 0.193 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Trials.} Each trial's stimulus level and group are drawn with
        replacement from the real experimental design, so every dataset keeps
        the real mix of the 8 stimulus levels and 4 groups. Responses are then
        simulated from the true curves.
  \item \textbf{Sizes.} 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, 8000 and 16{,}000 trials in
        total (about 60 to 4{,}000 per group). The real data, with 12{,}937 trials,
        lie between the two largest sizes.
  \item \textbf{Nested datasets.} Within a replicate, the size-$N$ dataset is
        the first $N$ trials of one long simulated run, so each larger dataset only
        adds trials to the smaller one. The trend with size is then not mixed
        with the noise of drawing a new dataset at every size.
  \item \textbf{Replicates.} The whole sweep was repeated 20 times with
        independent simulated data (140 fits). Each size and parameter therefore
        has $20 \times 4 = 80$ fits from which to estimate coverage. A calibrated
        model would give a coverage between 0.90 and 0.99 in 95\% of such
        cells, and about 0.95 on average.
\end{itemize}

An earlier run used only 3 replicates (12 fits per cell). Its coverage of the PSE
dropped to 0.67--0.75 between 1{,}000 and 4{,}000 trials, almost entirely
because of one replicate whose left\_uni PSE stayed about 3 posterior sds above
the truth. With 20 replicates that pattern does not recur (see below), so it was
chance in one simulated dataset.

\section{Results}

Every figure and number in this report is produced by \code{Convergence\_A.py};
the cell ``Numbers for Convergence\_A\_Report.txt'' prints the numbers that
the earlier cells do not, and also runs on the saved results without refitting.

\subsection{Sampling diagnostics}

All 140 fits sampled well: $\hat{R} \le 1.006$, a bulk ESS of at least 1{,}554
for every parameter, and a single divergence in total (one fit at 1{,}000
trials).

Every coverage value lies in the calibrated range 0.90--0.99
(Table~\ref{tab:coverage}), except for a few values of 1.00 ($\gamma_h$,
$\gamma_l$ and $\beta_1$ at the smallest sizes, and $\beta_1$ at 4{,}000
trials): there the HDIs are wider than needed, because the prior still
contributes. The 95\% HDIs of Model A can therefore be taken at face value
at the size of the real data.

Within single groups (20 fits per value) one value stands out: the coverage of
the right\_bi PSE at 16{,}000 trials is 0.75 (15 of 20), with $\beta_0$ for the
same group at 0.85. With 168 group-level coverage values, one this low can
happen by chance, and the same group is at 0.95 at 8{,}000 trials. But at
16{,}000 trials its PSE error (RMSE 0.018) is somewhat larger than its posterior
sd (0.014), so there may be a mild undercoverage of the PSE for steep curves at
large sizes. At a slope of 6 the curve rises over less than one stimulus spacing,
so only the two levels around the PSE carry information about it.

PSE, JND, $\beta_0$ and $\gamma_h$ are recovered without meaningful bias
(within about 0.3 posterior sd at every size, Table~\ref{tab:bias}). Only 7 of
the 3{,}360 parameter estimates (0.2\%) were more than 3 posterior sds from the
truth, as expected by chance.

\paragraph{$\gamma_l$ is estimated too high.} The true values (0.005 and 0.01)
lie in the lower tail of the prior, whose mean is 0.05, and the data contain
very few lapses of this kind: at 16{,}000 trials a group with $\gamma_l = 0.005$
has about 4{,}000 trials, but only 13\% (left\_uni) to 37\% (right\_uni) of
them are at levels where the curve is near its ceiling (above 0.95), so the data
contain only about 3 to 7 such lapses. The prior therefore pulls the
estimates up, by about one posterior sd at small sizes, decreasing to 0.36 sd at
16{,}000 trials (Figure~\ref{fig:gaml}). The posteriors are wide enough that
coverage stays at nominal.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{recovery_gam_l_mean_hdi_vs_size.png}
\caption{Posterior mean and 95\% HDI of $\gamma_l$ against dataset size, for the

\label{fig:gaml}

\paragraph{left\_uni: $\gamma_l$ and slope trade off.} The bias is largest and
most persistent for left\_uni: at 16{,}000 trials its $\gamma_l$ averages 0.015
against a true 0.005 (still 1.1 posterior sds too high), and its slope $\beta_1$
is 0.12 too high (0.6 sd). The left\_uni curve only reaches about 0.97 at the
highest stimulus, so the data cannot tell whether the remaining shortfall is a
lapse ($\gamma_l$) or a curve that has not yet reached its ceiling (the slope),
and a higher $\gamma_l$ is compensated by a steeper slope. Model B showed the
same problem for the same group. For the other three groups the $\gamma_l$ bias
falls to 0.3 sd or less by 16{,}000 trials. The slope bias at small sizes
(1.3--1.5 too high at 250 trials for both left-hand groups) comes from the
prior mean of 4 pulling the true slope of 3 upward.

All posteriors narrow steadily with more data (Figure~\ref{fig:sd},
Table~\ref{tab:sd}). PSE and $\beta_0$ narrow at the $1/\sqrt{N}$ rate over the
whole range, so for them the data dominate from 250 trials on. $\beta_1$, JND

contributes. $\beta_1$, JND and $\gamma_l$ reach the $1/\sqrt{N}$ rate from
about 4{,}000 trials; $\gamma_h$ is still a little slower at 16{,}000.

At 16{,}000 trials the posterior sd of the PSE is 0.018 standardized units
(0.27 $\mu m$) and that of the JND 0.014 (0.21 $\mu m$). Scaled to the real data
(about 0.8 times as many trials per group) these become about 0.30 and
0.23 $\mu m$.

\section{Conclusions for the real data}

At the size of the real data (12{,}937 trials, about 3{,}230 per group):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reliable:} the PSE and JND of every group, $\beta_0$, and
        $\gamma_h$: unbiased, with calibrated 95\% HDIs. The posterior sd is about
        0.3 $\mu m$ for the PSE and 0.2 $\mu m$ for the JND.
  \item \textbf{Reliable but with conservative HDIs:} $\beta_1$; its coverage is
        0.98--0.99.
  \item \textbf{Biased upward:} $\gamma_l$, by roughly 0.4--0.5 posterior sd
        overall, and by about one posterior sd for a group whose curve does not
        reach its ceiling within the stimulus range (left\_uni in the
        simulation). The HDIs still cover the truth, but the posterior means of
        small $\gamma_l$ should not be read literally.
  \item \textbf{Possibly slightly undercovered:} the PSE of groups with very steep
        curves (slope about 6), based on a single cell (right\_bi at 16{,}000
        trials); worth checking with more replicates or other true values.
  \item These conclusions hold only at the one set of true values tested, and
        Model A assumes the curve is the same in every session. When the real
        sessions differ (as the Model B fit shows they do in PSE), Model A's
        HDIs will be too narrow, because between-session variation is treated as
        trial noise.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Possible follow-ups.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Give Model A design-based priors as was done for Model B (in particular
        a lapse prior centered nearer 0.01 and a positive slope prior), with
        prior predictive checks, and rerun this study.
  \item Draw the true parameters from the prior for each replicate
        (simulation-based calibration), so calibration is tested across the
        whole parameter range rather than at one point.
  \item Check whether the right\_bi PSE undercoverage at 16{,}000 trials holds
        up with more replicates.
\end{enumerate}
