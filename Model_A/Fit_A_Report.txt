\section{Summary}

Model A (one psychometric curve per group, with all sessions pooled) was fit to
all 12{,}937 trials of the Sirius data (42 sessions, 4 groups). The main results:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The distractor lowers the PSE} by $-4.19$ $\mu m$
        [$-4.96$, $-3.44$] for the left hand and $-6.07$ $\mu m$ [$-6.85$, $-5.30$]
        for the right hand.
  \item \textbf{The distractor raises $\gamma_h$}, the rate of ``high''
        responses to the weakest stimuli, by about 0.04 for both hands.
  \item \textbf{The distractor raises the JND} for the left hand
        (+0.83 $\mu m$ [0.27, 1.37]); for the right hand the HDI includes 0. It
        has no clear effect on $\gamma_l$.
  \item These effects agree closely with Model B. The PSE shift is the same
        in both models to within 0.1 $\mu m$.
  \item \textbf{Model B fits the data much better.} Leave-one-out
        cross-validation favors it by 164 (SE 19) in expected log predictive
        density. Within single sessions, near the threshold, the observed
        responses vary more than Model A predicts. Model A therefore describes
        these 42 sessions pooled together; to generalize to a new session,
        Model B's population-level estimates are the appropriate ones.
\end{itemize}

Every figure and number in this report is produced by
\code{Main\_Analysis\_A.py}; the cell ``Numbers for Fit\_A\_Report.txt'' prints
the numbers that the earlier cells do not. All intervals are 95\% highest
density intervals (HDIs). PSE and JND are in micrometres of stimulus amplitude:
$\text{PSE}_{\mu m} = 28 + 14.63\,\text{PSE}$ and
$\text{JND}_{\mu m} = 14.63\,\text{JND}$, where 28 and 14.63 are the mean and sd
of the 8 amplitude levels.

\section{Model and Fit}

The model is the one described in the Model A convergence report. Each group
$g$ (left\_uni, left\_bi, right\_uni, right\_bi) has one psychometric curve,
\[
  \psi(x) = \gamma_{h,g} + (1 - \gamma_{h,g} - \gamma_{l,g})\,\invlogit(\beta_{0,g} + \beta_{1,g}\,x),
\]
shared by all its trials regardless of session, with the priors
$\beta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 6)$, $\beta_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(4, 2)$ and
$\gamma_h, \gamma_l = 0.25\,w$ with $w \sim \text{Beta}(\text{mean } 0.2,
\text{ sd } 0.15)$. These priors predate the design-based priors of Model B.
The lapse prior has mean 0.05, well above the lapse rates found here, but the
data dominate it: the posterior sd of each lapse rate is 5--27\% of its prior
sd, and the PSE and JND posteriors are far narrower than their priors
(Figure~\ref{fig:prior_post_pse}, Appendix~\ref{app:figs}).

The groups have 3{,}230--3{,}237 trials each. The fit used NUTS with 4 chains
of 1000 tuning and 5000 kept draws (20{,}000 draws in total) and took 2.4
minutes.

\subsection{Sampling diagnostics}

There were no divergences, and the tree depth never exceeded 5. All 40
parameters have $\hat{R} = 1.000$, bulk ESS $\ge 15{,}213$ and tail ESS
$\ge 8{,}802$. The chains overlap completely (Figure~\ref{fig:trace}).

The distractor shifts the PSE down by 4--6 $\mu m$ and raises $\gamma_h$ by
about 0.04, both with probability essentially 1 (Table~\ref{tab:effect},
Figures~\ref{fig:uni_bi} and~\ref{fig:effect}): with a distractor on the other
hand the monkey reports ``high'' at lower amplitudes, and occasionally even for
the weakest stimuli. The JND rises by 0.83 $\mu m$ for the left hand
($P > 0$: 0.998); for the right hand the rise of 0.27 $\mu m$ is uncertain
($P > 0$: 0.84). Any effect on $\gamma_l$ is small and uncertain.

\section{Model Checks and Comparison with Model B}

\subsection{Posterior predictive checks}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{ppc_pooled_by_stimulus.png}
\caption{Observed proportion of ``high'' responses at each amplitude, pooled
over sessions (black), against the posterior predictive median and 95\%
interval (blue). The dashed line is the trained threshold.}
\label{fig:ppc}
\end{figure}

Pooled over sessions, all 32 group-by-amplitude proportions lie inside their
95\% posterior predictive intervals (Figure~\ref{fig:ppc}): one curve per
group describes the pooled data well.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ppc_session_cells.png}
\caption{Posterior predictive check within single sessions. Left: PIT values of
the observed number of ``high'' responses in each session $\times$ group
$\times$ amplitude cell (flat at 1 for a calibrated model). Right: fraction of
the 42 sessions whose observed count lies inside its 95\% predictive interval,
by group and amplitude.}
\label{fig:ppc_sessions}
\end{figure}

Within single sessions the picture is different (Figure~\ref{fig:ppc_sessions}).
Model A predicts the same curve in every session, so it treats the variation
between sessions as if it were trial noise. Overall, 1{,}293 of the 1{,}344
session $\times$ group $\times$ amplitude cells (96.2\%) lie inside their 95\%
intervals, close to nominal, but 4.5\% of the PIT values are below 0.025 and
4.0\% above 0.975, against 2.5\% each for a calibrated model: the session counts
vary more than Model A predicts. The excess is concentrated at 32 $\mu m$, the
level just above the threshold, where the curve is steepest and a small shift
of a session's PSE changes the response rate most: there only 79\% (right\_uni)
and 86\% (left\_uni) of sessions lie inside their intervals. This matches the
Model B fit, which found the right-hand unimanual PSE drifting upward over the
study. For comparison, Model B, which gives each session its own curve, has
99.8\% of these cells inside their intervals.

\subsection{Leave-one-out cross-validation}

Model A has $\text{elpd}_\text{loo} = -3344.0$ (SE 64.5) with
$p_\text{loo} = 13.4$ and every Pareto $k$ at most 0.07. Model B has
$-3180.0$ (SE 64.3) with $p_\text{loo} = 179$. Both were fit to the same trials
in the same order, so the difference can be computed trial by trial: Model B is
better by 164.1 (SE 19.2), more than eight standard errors. Model B's 179
effective parameters against Model A's 13 are more than paid for by its better
predictions: the sessions really differ.

\subsection{Comparison of the estimates}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Posterior means of the group quantities: Model A (sessions pooled)
against the average of Model B's 42 session estimates.}
\label{tab:A_vs_B}
\begin{tabular}{lcccccccc}
\toprule
 & \multicolumn{2}{c}{PSE ($\mu m$)} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{JND ($\mu m$)} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$\gamma_h$} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$\gamma_l$} \\
 & A & B & A & B & A & B & A & B \\
\midrule
left\_uni  & 32.58 & 32.45 & 3.56 & 3.31 & 0.002 & 0.002 & 0.009 & 0.010 \\
left\_bi   & 28.38 & 28.16 & 4.39 & 4.34 & 0.041 & 0.027 & 0.016 & 0.010 \\
right\_uni & 31.57 & 31.44 & 4.09 & 3.57 & 0.003 & 0.003 & 0.006 & 0.008 \\
right\_bi  & 25.50 & 25.38 & 4.36 & 4.06 & 0.045 & 0.038 & 0.012 & 0.011 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The PSEs agree to within 0.22 $\mu m$ (Table~\ref{tab:A_vs_B}). The JNDs of
Model A are larger, by 0.05--0.52 $\mu m$, most for right\_uni. This is what
pooling does: averaging curves whose PSEs differ from session to session gives
a shallower curve than any single session has, so the pooled JND mixes the
monkey's precision within a session with the drift of its PSE between
sessions. Model B separates the two. Model A's bimanual $\gamma_h$ is also
higher (0.041 against 0.027 for left\_bi), probably for a related reason: in
sessions whose PSE is unusually low, ``high'' responses at the weakest
stimuli are more common, and the pooled curve can only absorb them as lapses.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{effect_A_vs_B.png}
\caption{Distractor effect under Model A (black), and under Model B averaged
over the 42 sessions (orange) and for a typical session (blue): posterior mean
and 95\% HDI.}
\label{fig:effect_AB}
\end{figure}

The distractor effects agree well (Figure~\ref{fig:effect_AB}). The PSE shift is
$-4.19$ (A) against $-4.29$ (B, average over sessions) for the left hand and
$-6.07$ against $-6.06$ for the right hand, with HDIs of nearly the same width
(about 1.5 $\mu m$). The $\gamma_h$ effect is somewhat larger under Model A
(0.038 against 0.025 for the left hand), and the JND effect somewhat smaller
(0.83 against 1.03 for the left hand, 0.27 against 0.49 for the right), but
the HDIs overlap widely and the conclusions are the same.

The difference between the models lies in what the estimates describe. Model
A's effects, like Model B's average over sessions, describe these 42 sessions.
For a new session from the same population, Model B's typical-session effect is
the relevant one, and its HDI is about twice as wide for the PSE (2.6 and
2.9 $\mu m$ against 1.5): the uncertainty about how sessions vary, which Model A
cannot express.

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The fit is reliable: no divergences, $\hat{R} = 1.000$, bulk ESS above
        15{,}000, and the pooled data are reproduced at every amplitude.
  \item The distractor lowers the PSE by 4.2 (left) and 6.1 (right) $\mu m$ and
        raises $\gamma_h$ by about 0.04; it raises the left-hand JND by about
        0.8 $\mu m$. These conclusions agree with Model B.
  \item Model A ignores the variation between sessions, which is real: Model B
        predicts the data much better (elpd difference 164, SE 19), and within
        single sessions the responses near the threshold vary more than Model A
        allows. As a result Model A's JNDs and bimanual $\gamma_h$ are somewhat
        inflated, and its HDIs describe only the sessions observed.
  \item Model A is a useful, fast summary of the pooled data and a check on
        Model B, but Model B should be used for inference.
\end{itemize}
